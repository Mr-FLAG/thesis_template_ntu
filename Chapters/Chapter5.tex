\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:prelim}
\subsection{Slack System in Single Site Case}

The slack system is developed to provide an intuitive protectiveness assessment of the current system state, identifying whether any jobs are projected to exceed their completion times under the PS policy. It is defined as follows \cite{friedman2003protective}:

Consider a single-site system with a homogeneous resource, where the total resource capacity is set to $1$ for simplicity. During the procession, the system hypothetically runs a Processor Sharing (PS) policy to obtain each job's PS completion time, which serves as its deadline. Therefore, every job in the system has two types of job loads, namely the real load and the PS load, and they are equal upon job arrival. The PS load then decreases according to a simulated execution of the PS policy, where processing capacity is evenly shared by all jobs with positive loads, while its real load keeps or decreases according to the actual scheduling policy. Let the current real loads and PS loads for $n$ jobs in the system be $w_1, w_2, \dots, w_n$ and $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$, respectively. For simplicity, the job sequence is sorted in ascending order of their PS loads (note that the PS load order is identical to these jobs' PS completion time order), i.e., $v_1 \le v_2 \le \dots \le v_n$; Then, the slack of a job $J_i$ is computed as follows:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:slack_single}
    s_i = \sum_{l=1}^{i} v_{l} + (n-i)v_i- \sum_{l=1}^i w_l.
\end{equation}
Specifically, the slack $s_i$ consists of two parts: (i) the virtual completion time of $J_i$'s PS load under the PS policy, given by $\sum_{l=1}^{i} v_{l} + (n-i)v_i$, and (ii) the time required to complete the real loads of the first $i$ jobs, denoted by $\sum_{l=1}^i w_l$. Consequently, $s_i$ quantifies the maximum permissible delay for $J_i$ and its preceding jobs to complete before violating a deadline set at $J_i$'s PS completion time.

\subsection{Two Types of Events in the Slack System}
\label{sec:Pre_TwoEvents}
After constructing the slack system, we can now identify how the slack system will change under various events. \cite{friedman2003protective} discussed the slack system change under two types of events. First, if the system serves some job $J_i$ for time $\Delta t$, the slacks of jobs before $J_i$ in the PS load order will decrease by $\Delta t$:
\begin{equation}
    s_l = s_l -\Delta t,\quad l = \{1, \dots, i-1\}
\end{equation}

Second, if a new job arrives in the system and joins in the sequence at the $i$-th position based on its PS load (suppose its initial load is $x$), it will cause slacks of jobs before it to increase by $x/u$:
\begin{equation}
    s_l = s_l + \frac{x}{u},\quad l = \{1, \dots, i-1\}
\end{equation}
In both events, slacks of subsequent jobs ($J_{i+1}, J_{i+2}, \dots, J_n$) keep the same. 

\subsection{Servable and Completable jobs}
Friedman \textit{et al.} \cite{friedman2003protective} proved that a scheduling policy is protective if and only if the slack of each job remains non-negative at all times. In other words, once the slack of any job becomes negative, then regardless of subsequent scheduling decisions (in the absence of new job arrivals), at least one job will inevitably complete later than its completion time under the PS policy. Therefore, the analysis of the first type of event indicates that not all jobs can be prioritized if the system aims to maintain protectiveness. This introduces the concept of servability and completability. Without loss of generality, we assume that the job sequence is sorted in ascending order of their PS loads: %For simplicity, in the following definition we assume that the job sequence is already sorted based on their PS loads.

%Consequently, avoiding negative slack implies that not all jobs can be prioritized for execution if the system aims to maintain protectiveness. \begin{definition}
    A job $J_i$ is \textbf{servable} if and only $s_{l} > 0$ for all $l \in \{1, \dots, i-1\}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    A job $J_i$ is \textbf{completable} if and only if $s_{l} \ge w_{i} / u$ for all $l \in \{1, \dots, i-1\}$.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Fair Sojourn Protocol (FSP), Optimistic FSP (OFSP) and Pessimistic FSP (PFSP)}
\cite{friedman2003protective} proposed three protective scheduling policies in the single site case, namely Fair Sojourn Protocol (FSP), Optimistic FSP (OFSP) and Pessimistic FSP (PFSP). FSP schedule jobs based on the PS load (completion time) order. OFSP and PFSP serve the servable and completable job of the least positive real load first, respectively. Specifically, OFSP serves the servable job of the least real load until it completes or becomes not servable, then repeat the computation of the servable job set to serve the next. The word "optimistic" means that although the job chosen by OFSP may not be able to serve to completion, the policy expects that there will be new job arrivals during its processing to further extend the PS completion time (and hence the slack) of the job, thus making it completable. On the contrary, the word "pessimistic" means that PFSP serves the job that is already completable (it stays completable if new job arrives, since we have discussed in Section \ref{sec:Pre_TwoEvents} that new job arrival will cause existing jobs' slack to keep or increase), which is more conservative and stable than OFSP.

%Note that the first job in the PS order is always servable and completable because the set $\{1, \dots, i-1\} = \emptyset$ for $i = 1$. In addition, a job with its real load completed while not removed from the system (i.e., its PS load is not completed yet) will have its slack set to infinity. This convention ensures the correctness of the system's computations regarding servable and completable jobs. In addition, the paper \cite{friedman2003protective} proved that a scheduling policy is protective if and only if the slack of each job is kept non-negative throughout the time span. In other words, once the slack of any job becomes negative, then no matter how the jobs are scheduled in the sequel (suppose no new job arrives), there must be at least one job who will finish later than its finish time under PS policy.

The following example illustrates how protective scheduling policies like FSP maintain long-term fairness and prevent job starvation compared to efficiency-targeted policies. Consider a job arrival sequence where two jobs, $J_x$ and $J_1$, arrive simultaneously at $t=0$ with initial loads $w_x=2$ and $w_1=1$, respectively. Thereafter, a new job $J_{a+1}$ with a load of $1$ arrives at each subsequent integer second $a$ (where $a \in \{1, 2, \dots, 100\}$).

Under an efficiency-targeted policy like Shortest Remaining Processing Time (SRPT), the system will serve the jobs in the order of $J_1, J_2, \dots, J_{101}$, and finally $J_x$. This occurs because each $J_a$ has a smaller load than $J_x$ and arrives exactly when the previous $J_{a-1}$ completes. Consequently, $J_x$ will complete at $t=103$, suffering from severe starvation. 

Suppose the system adopts FSP instead. The system execution will proceed as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{$t=0$:} The system has $J_x$ and $J_1$ with initial loads of $2$ and $1$, respectively. The system prioritizes $J_1$.
    
    \item \textbf{$t=1$:} $J_1$ finishes its real execution, and $J_2$ arrives. Meanwhile, in the virtual PS policy the remaining PS loads are $v_x=1.5$, $v_1=0.5$, and $v_2=1$. Since $J_1$ completes and $J_2$ has smaller PS load than $J_x$, the system chooses to serve $J_2$.
    
    \item \textbf{$t=2$:} $J_2$ finishes its real execution, and $J_3$ arrives. The PS loads are updated to $v_x \approx 1.167$, $v_1 \approx 0.167$, $v_2 \approx 0.667$, and $v_3=1$. The system then schedules $J_3$.
    
    \item \textbf{$t=3$:} $J_3$ finishes its real execution, and $J_4$ arrives. Notably, during the interval $[2, 3]$, $J_1$ also completes its PS load and is removed from the system. Therefore, the remaining PS loads become $v_x \approx 0.889$, $v_2 \approx 0.389$, $v_3 \approx 0.722$, and $v_4=1$.
\end{itemize}

At the time $t=3$, there are four jobs remaining in the system and two of them still have real loads ($J_x$ and $J_4$). Then, the system calculates that $J_x$ has a lower remaining PS load ($0.889 < 1$), so FSP preempts the pattern and schedules $J_x$, preventing it from indefinite starvation.

\section{System Model}
\label{sec:model}
Consider a distributed computing environment comprising $m$ distinct sites denoted by $\mathcal{S} = \{S_1, S_2, \dots, S_m\}$. Each site $S_j$ has computing resources with capacity $u_j$. A set of $n$ jobs $J_1, J_2, ..., J_n$ is submitted to the system. Each job $J_i$ consists of workloads across sites that can be processed in parallel, and its overall completion time is determined by the last finishing workload. %For simplicity, we use $[x]$ to represent the set $\{1, 2, \dots, x\}$ for a positive integer $x$. 
We describe the real load distribution of the jobs using a load matrix $W_{m\times n}$\footnote{Throughout this paper, we use capital letters to represent matrices and their corresponding lowercase letters to denote the elements therein.}. Specifically, each element $w_{ji}$ represents the processing load of job $J_i$ at site $S_j$. For example, if $u_2=2$ and $w_{21}=4$, processing $J_1$'s load at $S_2$ will require $w_{21}/u_2=2$ time units. Furthermore, the system simulates a Processor Sharing (PS) policy at each site to estimate both the site-specific and global PS completion times for every job. Under this simulated policy, each site equally shares its capacity among all active jobs (i.e., those with positive remaining loads). To track this process, we introduce a virtual load matrix $V_{m\times n}$, where each element $v_{ji}$ denotes the virtual load of job $J_i$ at site $S_j$ under the PS policy, henceforth referred to as the PS load. The key notations used in this paper are summarized in Table \ref{tab:Notation}. We aim to design scheduling policies that achieve the highest job processing efficiency possible in terms of average job response time, while ensuring that every job can complete before it virtually completes under the PS policy. Note that although the model is formulated in a static setting, it can be extended to online environments by capturing snapshots of dynamic job load distributions.

\section{Distributed Slack System}
\label{sec:distributed_slack}
In this section, we discuss how to build a slack system in the distributed job execution case that assists us in identifying the protectiveness of the system and designing algorithms. There are two types of views constructing the slack system: a global view and an independent view.

\subsection{Slack System in Multiple Site Case: A Global View}
Since a job's global PS completion time is determined by the maximum of its site-specific PS completion times across all sites, a natural idea to extend the slack system to the distributed job execution case is to use each job's global PS completion time as the sorting criteria, which we refer to as the Global Slack ($GS$) scheme.

In the single site case, computing the PS completion time requires jobs to be sorted in ascending order of their PS loads. However, in the distributed case site-specific PS load distribution $V$ may cause different job sequences at different sites. For notational simplicity, let $P_j(i)$ denote the set of jobs that precede (and include) job $J_i$ when sorted in ascending order of their site-specific PS loads on site $S_j$:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:P_j(i)}
    P_j(i) = \bigl\{ J_k \in \mathcal{J} \;\big|\; (v_{jk}, k) \le (v_{ji}, i) \bigr\}.
\end{equation}
Here $\le$ denotes the standard lexicographical order on the tuples. In addition, different sites $S_j$'s in the system may have different resource capacities $u_j$'s. Thus, the site-specific PS completion time of a job $J_i$ at a site $S_j$ can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{u_j}\left(\sum_{J_l \in P_j(i)}v_{jl} + (n - |P_j(i)|)v_{ji} \right).
\end{equation}
Therefore, the global PS completion time $PS_i$ of a job $J_i$ is the maximum of site-specific PS completion times of $J_i$ across $m$ sites:
\begin{equation}
    PS_i = \max_{0\le j \le m} \frac{1}{u_j}\left(\sum_{J_l \in P_j(i)}v_{jl} + (n - |P_j(i)|)v_{ji}\right).
\end{equation}

Based on this, we can sort jobs in increasing order of their global PS completion times, i.e., $PS_1 \le PS_2 \le \dots \le PS_n$. After sorting, the global slack of a job $J_i$ at site $S_j$ under the $GS$ scheme can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    s_{ji} = PS_i - \frac{1}{u_j}\sum_{l=1}^i w_{jl}.
\end{equation}

Under the $GS$ scheme, a scheduling policy is protective if and only if all $s_{ji}$'s are non-negative during the system procession. Similarly to the single site case (introduced in Section \ref{sec:Pre_TwoEvents}), serving job $J_i$ at site $S_j$ for a duration of $\Delta t$ reduces the slack of all preceding jobs ($P_j(i)\setminus J_i$) at $S_j$ by $\Delta t$, while new job arrival will only increase the slack of jobs in $P_j(i)\setminus J_i$ at $S_j$. Therefore, the definitions of servable and completable jobs are also applied to the distributed case, except that they are site-specific. We define that a job is \textbf{servable} or \textbf{completable} globally if and only if it is servable or completable at all sites. Note that the completion of a job's real load at a given site does not affect its global servability or completability. In addition, the first job in the global PS order is always servable and completable globally because the index set $\{1, \dots, i-1\}$ is empty for $i = 1$.

\subsection{Conflict of Globally Order-based Scheduling to Protectiveness under the GS Scheme}
\label{sec:conflict}
An ideal job schedule naturally desires a global order that is consistent across all sites, which is intuitive, simple and guarantees efficiency (obviously policies that prioritize different jobs at different sites will lead to poor job completion time). In the protective scheduling, this requires the algorithm outputs a global job order that every job is completable globally when it comes to procession. However, we prove that under the $GS$ scheme the protectiveness cannot be guaranteed if the system schedules jobs using a global job order. The primary reason is that during the procession, while the $GS$ scheme ensures that the deadline derived from the global PS completion time of each job is maintained, the PS completion time of some job $J_i$ at some site $S_j$ can be violated. Since job arrivals are online, it is possible that after a set of new jobs' arrival this violated site $S_j$ now decides the global PS completion time of job $J_i$, thus causing the negative site-specific slack $s_{ji}$ to be the global slack and consequently violates the protectiveness. The example below further illustrates this point. %the $IS$ scheme does not guarantee that such a globally completable job exists (so the system may not be able to find a valid schedule), and

Consider a distributed system of two sites, both with capacity $1$. Suppose two jobs $J_1$, $J_2$ are submitted to the system with initial load distribution $W = \begin{bmatrix} 2a & 2a+2e \\ 2a+e & 2a-e \end{bmatrix}$, where $a \gg e > 0$. The site-specific PS completion time can be computed as $\begin{bmatrix} 4a & 4a+2e \\ 4a & 4a-2e \end{bmatrix}$, thus the global PS completion time of the two jobs are $4a, 4a+2e$, respectively, which introduces the global PS order $J_1 \prec J_2$.

We compute the slack system based on the $GS$ scheme: $J_1$ is the first job in the global PS order, so its global slack is $[4a - 2a=2a, 4a-(2a+e)=2a-e]$; $J_2$ is the second and its global slack is $[4a+2e-2a - (2a+2e) =0, 4a+2e-(2a+e)-(2a-e)=2e]$, resulting in $S = \begin{bmatrix} 2a & 0 \\ 2a-e & 2e \end{bmatrix}$. In this case, $J_1$ is the first job in the global PS order and is globally servable and completable, while $J_2$ is not completable because $s_{11} = 2a < w_{12} = 2a+2e$. Therefore, any scheduling policy that wants to derive a global job order while keeping the system protective will serve $J_1$ to completion first. Meanwhile, if we focus on the load distribution at site $S_2$ solely, another type of slack can be calculated (we define it as "local slack" to differentiate it from the site-specific slack under the $GS$ scheme) by treating $S_2$ as a single site: the site-specific PS completion times of the two jobs are $[4a(J_1), 4a-2e(J_2)]$ and the local PS load order is $J_2 \prec J_1$; Based on this local PS loads and order, the local slack at site $S_2$ is $[4a-(2a-e)-(2a+e)=0(J_1), 4a-2e-(2a-e)=2a-e(J_2)]$, which indicates that $J_2$ is locally completable at site $S_2$ while $J_1$ is not, and serving $J_1$ will deteriorate $J_2$'s local slack.

Suppose the system adopts the order $J_1 \prec J_2$. At $t=2a$, the real load and PS load distribution are $W = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2a+2e \\ e & 2a-e \end{bmatrix}$ and $V = \begin{bmatrix} a & a+2e \\ a+e & a-e \end{bmatrix}$, respectively. The $GS$ slack does not change because the system serves the first job in the global PS order, and it is still protective. Meanwhile, in the local slack at site $S_2$, serving $J_1$ continuously decreases the slack of $J_2$ and its local slack becomes negative ($2a-e-2a=-e$) already.

Suppose at $t=2a$ a new job $J_3$ arrives with the load distribution $[0, a-2e]$. At this time, the real load and PS load distribution becomes $W = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2a+2e & 0 \\ e & 2a-e & a-2e \end{bmatrix}$ and $V = \begin{bmatrix} a & a+2e & 0 \\ a+e & a-e & a-2e \end{bmatrix}$, respectively. Therefore, the PS completion time is $\begin{bmatrix} 2a & 2a+2e & 0 \\ 3a-2e & 3a-4e & 3a-6e \end{bmatrix}$. In addition, since $a \gg e$, the global PS completion time of all three jobs are decided at site $S_2$, and the global PS order becomes $J_3 \prec J_2 \prec J_1$, and the $GS$ slack is $S = \begin{bmatrix} a-4e & a-6e & 3a-6e \\ 0 & -e & 2a-4e \end{bmatrix}$, i.e., the protectiveness is violated at the arrival of $J_3$. %Here $J_3$ and $J_2$ have the same global PS completion time, but exchanging them in the global PS order derives the same conclusion.

Furthermore, as long as the site-specific slack of some job $J_i$ at some site $S_j$ becomes negative, we can always assume a set of simultaneous job arrivals $\{J_{(1)}, J_{(2)}, \dots\}$ who all have load $x \le v_{ji}$ at $S_j$ and $0$ load at other sites. This limitation suggests two potential paradigms for designing protective policies: (i) resorting to order-independent strategies (such as sharing system capacity among a specific set of jobs or interleaving job execution via time-slicing), which increases implementation complexity and may degrade overall processing efficiency, or (ii) introducing another scheme of slack upon which more elegant scheduling mechanisms can be robustly established.

As demonstrated by the preceding example, globally order-based scheduling policies fail to preserve the protectiveness in distributed environments under the $GS$ scheme. Furthermore, if the site-specific slack of any job $J_i$ at a site $S_j$ becomes negative, one can systematically construct a worst-case scenario involving the simultaneous arrival of a job set $\{J_{(1)}, J_{(2)}, \dots, J_{(z)}\}$, each containing a load $x \le v_{ji}$ at $S_j$ and zero load at all other sites. Under such conditions, the PS completion time of $J_i$ at $S_j$ can be arbitrarily delayed while its site-specific slack keeps negative (i.e., event type $2$ in Section \ref{sec:Pre_TwoEvents}). For a sufficiently large $z$, this prolonged site-specific completion time will become the global PS completion time of $J_i$, thereby rendering its $GS$ slack at $S_j$ negative. This fundamental limitation highlights two prospective paradigms for the design of protective policies: (i) adopting order-independent strategies—such as proportional capacity allocation among a designated job subset or time-slicing execution—which inevitably introduces implementation overhead and may compromise aggregate processing efficiency; or (ii) formulating an alternative slack scheme that facilitates the robust design of protective scheduling mechanisms. %\textcolor{blue}{This fundamental limitation indicates a stronger constraint requirement on the slack system in order to preserve protectiveness, which we will introduce next.}

\subsection{Slack System in Multiple Site Case: An Independent View}
Another natural approach of extending the slack system to the distributed environment is to compute the slack at each site independently, which we refer to as the Independent Slack ($IS$) scheme. Under the $IS$ scheme, there is no global PS order, and each site performs slack calculations independently. Retaining the notation $P_j(i)$ for the set of jobs preceding (and including) $J_i$ based on their site-specific PS loads at $S_j$, the slack of $J_i$ at site $S_j$ under the $IS$ scheme is given by:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:slack_IS}
    s_{ji} = \frac{1}{u_j}\left(\sum_{J_l \in P_j(i)}v_{jl} + (n - |P_j(i)|)v_{ji} - \sum_{J_l \in P_j(i)}w_{jl}\right).
\end{equation}

Similar to the $GS$ scheme, a protective algorithm in the $IS$ scheme requires that every $s_{ji}$ to be non-negative during system procession, i.e., the actual completion time of each site-specific job at its site does not exceed its site-specific PS completion time. Compared with the $GS$ scheme, $IS$ scheme appears to be a stricter constraint since the completion deadline is set at each site rather than a single global one. Consequently, the global completion time of each job is also bounded by its global PS completion time, and protectiveness is guaranteed. The main drawback of the $IS$ scheme is that scheduling policies following it may prioritize different jobs at different sites (i.e., a global scheduling order may not exist), leading to poor efficiency in terms of overall job response time. 


\section{PFSP\_x: A Trade-off Algorithm Framework}
\label{sec:PFSP_x}
Through the above analysis, we have shown the dilemma in distributed protective scheduling: adopting the $GS$ scheme can achieve higher efficiency by deriving global job scheduling orders, but it may violate the protectiveness. Meanwhile, using the $IS$ scheme can guarantee the protectiveness, but may lead to poor efficiency because it may prioritize different jobs at different sites. The key to solving this dilemma is to keep the $IS$ slack of each job at each site non-negative while adjusting the schedule to approximate the global job order that is ideal for efficiency.% Based on this insight, we propose a novel heuristic algorithm framework PFSP\_x which combines the independent slack system with any globally order-based scheduling policy to enhance efficiency while maintaining protectiveness.

\subsection{PFSP\_x}

At a single site, PFSP\_x computes each job's slack under the $IS$ scheme $s_i$ and the minimum slack of preceding jobs of each job $r_i$ (lines 3-6), respectively. Because computing the slack requires the cumulative sum of preceding jobs' PS load and real load, a temporary variable $\sigma$ is used to record the sum and decrease the computing complexity. Note that a job $J_i$ is completable if and only if its real load $w_i$ is smaller or equal to the slack of all its preceding jobs, which is identical to $r_i \ge w_i$. Next, the algorithm iteratively calculates the set of completable jobs $\mathcal{C}$ (line 8) by judging whether $r_k \ge w_k$ for each unscheduled job. Then, it schedules the job with the highest priority in the hint order (line 9). Here, we define $\text{pos}(J_i, \mathbf{h})$ as the position (index) of job $J_i$ within $\mathbf{h}$, where $\mathbf{h}$ is the permutation (hint order) of jobs in $\mathcal{J}$ given as input. A lower index indicates a higher priority. Then, the algorithm appends the selected job $J_y$ to the schedule of site $S_j$ (line 10) and removes $J_y$ from consideration (line 11). After scheduling $J_y$, the algorithm updates all $s_i$'s and $r_i$'s, respectively (line 12-16). For each job before $J_y$ in the PS load order, its slack decreases by $w_y$; for $J_y$, $s_y$ becomes infinity as specified; for each job after $J_y$, its slack keeps and requires no operation. After updating all the slacks, the algorithm updates $r_i$'s as well. The iteration repeats until all jobs are scheduled at site $S_j$, then it moves to the next site and continues the same process until all sites are processed. Finally, the algorithm returns $m$ job execution orders corresponding to each site. In this way, PFSP\_x achieves both the protectiveness guarantee by following the $IS$ scheme and the efficiency improvement by approximating the hint order derived from any efficiency-targeted scheduling policy.

\subsection{Complexity Analysis}
The outer loop of the algorithm iterates over $m$ sites. In each iteration, sorting $n$ jobs in ascending order of site-specific PS loads takes $O(n\log n)$ time. To efficiently compute the slack of each job, we maintain and reuse a running cumulative sum, denoted as $\sigma$, which aggregates the PS loads and real loads of the job itself and all its predecessors. Consequently, evaluating each slack $s_i$ and the minimum preceding slack $r_i$ can be achieved in $O(1)$ time. This reduces the total time complexity for the initial slack calculation of all $n$ jobs to $O(n \log n)$. Next, the algorithm repeats $n$ times, in which it computes the set of locally completable jobs $\mathcal{C}$ and searches for the completable job with the highest priority in the hint order $\mathbf{h}$. The former operation requires checking whether $r_k \ge w_k$ for each unscheduled job $J_k$, which takes $O(n)$ time. Selecting the job with the highest priority in $\mathbf{h}$ also takes $O(n)$ time. Updating the slack $s_i$ and minimum preceding slack $r_i$ of all jobs both take $O(n)$ time. Therefore, the overall time complexity of PFSP\_x is $O(m(n \log n + n^2)) = O(mn^2)$. Furthermore, since the computation at each site can be executed fully in parallel, the local time complexity at each individual site is reduced to $O(n^2)$.

\subsection{Deployment}
\label{sec:deploy}
The PFSP\_x algorithm can be deployed at a central scheduler or at each site of the real-world distributed computing environments to enhance job scheduling efficiency while ensuring fairness. Initially, the system requires collecting the real load (and PS load if necessary) distribution information of each job across sites, in which it runs the efficiency-targeted scheduling policy to derive the hint job order $\mathbf{h}$. If the system deploys PFSP\_x at the central scheduler, the scheduler will compute the $IS$ slack and the scheduling order of all sites, then it propagates each derived order $X_{j,*}$ to its corresponding site $S_j$ for execution. Also, PFSP\_x can be deployed at each site independently, in which the central scheduler only needs to compute and propagate the hint order $\mathbf{h}$ to all sites. After receiving $\mathbf{h}$, each site can compute the local $IS$ slack and its scheduling order independently. The system recomputes only when new job arrives, and old jobs are removed from schedule when they complete both in real load and PS load. Note that if the computation of the $IS$ slack and the scheduling decisions is performed locally at each site, it will effectively reduce the communication overhead and allow for efficient scheduling in large distributed systems. %In either way, the hint order $h$ is derived from an efficiency-targeted scheduling policy that considers the global load distribution of jobs across sites, which guides the local scheduling decisions towards higher efficiency while maintaining protectiveness through the independent slack system.

\subsection{System Configuration}
We model a distributed environment comprising $m$ identical sites, where each site is provisioned with an equal capacity. To evaluate our algorithms under varying degrees of data locality and skewness, we initialize the initial load distribution of each job by deploying its tasks among the sites based on the Zipf distribution and summing up their task processing times. Specifically, the task allocation across sites follows the proportional weights $1, 2^{-\alpha}, 3^{-\alpha}, \dots, m^{-\alpha}$, where the skewness parameter $\alpha$ controls the load imbalance. A higher $\alpha$ value dictates that tasks are more heavily concentrated on a handful of premium sites, while $\alpha = 0$ refers to a uniform distribution. We evaluate four distinct skew profiles by varying $\alpha \in \{0, 0.5, 1, 2\}$. To prevent global load hotspots and maintain long-term workload balance across the cluster, the site=specific load mapping order is randomly shuffled for each job. Additionally, the baseline arrival timestamps from the trace are linearly scaled to emulate diverse system utilizations. While we examined various combinations of site scales, slot capacities, and load levels—all yielding consistent performance behaviors—we restrict our focus to a representative setup of $m = 10$ sites, $32$ slots per site, and $50\%$, $60\%$, $70\%$, and $80\%$ system utilizations due to page constraints.

\subsection{Baseline policies}
We also implemented the following baseline policies in the experiments for comparison. The first two are efficiency-targeted policies, namely Shortest Remaining Processing Time (SRPT) and Workload-Aware Greedy Scheduling (SWAG) \cite{hung2015scheduling}. SRPT executes jobs based on their completion time in the case that there is no scheduled workload in the system. Under our system model, the completion time of a job $J_i$ is computed as $\max_{j \in[m]} w_{ji}/u_j$ if the system is idle, and SRPT derives the job execution order by sorting these completion times from low to high. SWAG schedules jobs in a heuristic manner by estimating their completion times based on scheduled workloads in the system. Once a job is scheduled, SWAG updates the job's load distribution to the system, and the new round of calculation of remaining jobs' completion times is based on the updated system workload. We present PFSP\_SWAG which uses the job execution order derived by SWAG as the hint order $\mathbf{h}$.

In addition, we extend these protective algorithms in the single site case (FSP, PFSP and OFSP) to the distributed case based on both of the slack schemes. Under the $IS$ scheme, we have FSP\_i, OFSP\_i and PFSP\_i, which runs FSP, OFSP and PFSP independently at each site. Similarly, under the $GS$ scheme, FSP\_g derives the global job execution order based on the global PS completion time, while OFSP\_g and PFSP\_g serve the globally servable or completable job that finishes the fastest if served first, respectively. Because the first job in the global PS order is always globally servable and completable, OFSP\_g and PFSP\_g is guaranteed to derive a valid schedule. However, they do not guarantee protectiveness, as in the counterexample of Section \ref{sec:conflict} FSP\_g, OFSP\_g and PFSP\_g all choose to serve $J_1$ to completion at first. %For all the policies mentioned, the average time of computing the solution during the simulation is around $10^{-5}$ seconds.

The following example further illustrates how FSP\_g, OFSP\_g and PFSP\_g works in the distributed job execution case. Consider a distributed system of two sites, both with capacity $1$. Suppose at the beginning $t=0$ two jobs $J_1$ and $J_2$ are submitted to the system. The initial load distribution of the jobs are $W = \begin{bmatrix} 8 & 10 \\ 9 & 7 \end{bmatrix}$, where the PS completion time of each job at each site is $\begin{bmatrix} 16 & 18 \\ 16 & 14 \end{bmatrix}$. Therefore, the global PS completion time of $J_1$ and $J_2$ are $16$ and $18$, respectively and the global PS order is $J_1 \prec J_2$. Therefore, FSP\_g will serve $J_1$ first then $J_2$ at all sites, and the average job response time is $(9+18)/2=13.5$.

We compute the slack system based on the $GS$ scheme: $J_1$ is the first job in the global PS order, so its global slack is $[16 - 8=8, 16-9=7]$; $J_2$ is the second and its global slack is $[18 - 10 - 8=0, 18-7-9=2]$, resulting in $S = \begin{bmatrix} 8 & 0 \\ 7 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$. In this case, $J_1$ is the first job in the global PS order and is globally servable and completable, while $J_2$ is not completable because $s_{11} = 8 < w_{12} = 10$. Therefore, PFSP\_g will behave the same as FSP\_g, i.e. serve $J_1$ to completion, then $J_2$. Meanwhile, $J_2$ is servable since its prior job $J_1$'s slack $s_{11}=8>0, s_{21}=7>0$ at all sites, and $J_2$ will finish at $t=10$ if it is served first while it is $t=9$ for job $J_1$. Therefore, although both jobs are servable, OFSP\_g will also serve $J_1$ to completion since it finishes earlier than $J_2$, and the average job response time is also $13.5$. 
%In this case, OFSP\_g can serve either of them. When $J_1$ finishes, OFSP\_g turns back to $J_2$ and serve it until $J_2$ finishes. This will result in the average job response time of $(16+17)/2=16.5$.

The average JRT is defined as the average time span from each job's arrival to its completion, where a lower average JRT indicates higher job processing efficiency of the policy. The number of violations to the PS deadline is defined as the number of jobs whose actual completion time exceeds their completion time under the PS schedule, which indicates the degree of long-term fairness in the system. A larger number of violations refers to that more jobs fail to finish before their completion times under the PS policy, indicating a higher degree of unfairness/starvation in job scheduling. \subsection{On inaccurate job load estimation}

All scheduling policies introduced above rely heavily on accurate estimations of job load distributions across sites. Inaccurate estimations inevitably lead to system performance degradation in both average job response time and PS deadline violations. Based on our evaluation environment with 10,000 jobs, when the estimated job load uniformly deviates within $\pm10\%$ of the ground truth (i.e., ranging from $90\%$ to $110\%$), the average job response time increases by $5\%$ to $12\%$, accompanied by up to hundreds of PS deadline violations across different scheduling policies. Estimation errors manifest in two distinct forms:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Over-estimation} assigns a job a larger estimated load than its actual load, which may deprioritize it in the execution sequence. However, because other concurrent jobs will either have higher priorities or keep the priorities unchanged, their protective scheduling bounds remain generally intact. Consequently, over-estimation predominantly impacts the over-estimated job itself, causing fewer performance degradations.
    
    \item \textbf{Under-estimation}, on the contrary, introduces severe systemic risks. When a job is underestimated, it accumulates a substantial amount of hidden workload and enters a "phantom state" once its estimated load is depleted. In the scheduler's logical model, this job is marked as completed and prioritized to clear the queue rapidly. In reality, however, its remaining unfulfilled workload continues to execute, inducing severe, unmodeled head-of-line blocking and starving subsequent jobs, which ultimately triggers a surge in PS deadline violations.
\end{itemize}

To counteract performance degradation under job load under-estimations, we propose a mitigation mechanism, namely Processor Sharing Fallback (PSF). PSF allocates a fair capacity share to these "phantom state" jobs: Once a job exhausts its estimated load while retaining unfulfilled real workload, it transitions into a fallback PS state. Under this state, the job receives a fixed equal share ($1/N_{\text{active}}$) of the site capacity, where $N_{\text{active}}$ represents the concurrent count of active jobs with remaining real load at current site. The leftover capacity is subsequently allocated to non-fallback jobs following the primary scheduling order.

Due to space limitations, we present representative results using the Alibaba 2018 trace under a 10\% deviation rate and 70\% system utilization here; full experimental results are shown in Appendix \ref{AppendixA}. The results indicate that policies under the $GS$ scheme (FSP\_g, PFSP\_g) inherently yield the lowest violation counts. Crucially, integrating the PSF mechanism significantly reduces violations across all policies, with our proposed protective scheduling policies under both $GS$ and $IS$ consistently outperforming efficiency-targeted baselines.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:conclusion3}

In this chapter, we investigated how to achieve the dual objective between efficiency and long-term fairness in geo-distributed job execution by extending the theory of protective scheduling in the single site case. We established the distributed slack system to help judge the system protectiveness using two perspectives: the Global Slack ($GS$) scheme and the Independent Slack ($IS$) scheme. Through theoretical analysis, we demonstrated a critical limitation that any globally order-based scheduling policy is incapable of guaranteeing strict protectiveness due to dynamic job arrivals. To overcome this dilemma, we proposed PFSP\_x, a novel heuristic scheduling framework that adopts the $IS$ scheme to ensure protectiveness while approximating a hint job order derived by any efficiency-targeted policies. We also developed a series of extension policies based on the single-site policies FSP, PFSP and OFSP as the baseline. Extensive trace-driven simulations using Alibaba cluster datasets demonstrated that our proposed PFSP\_SWAG policy achieves the highest job processing efficiency among all strictly protective policies. Furthermore, the extension policy PFSP\_g under the $GS$ scheme is proved capable of delivering near-optimal average job response times with negligible fairness violations. In conclusion, we present two primary scheduling policies, by which the distributed system is empowered to flexibly adapt to different system requirements, effectively balancing the delicate trade-off between job processing efficiency and long-term job fairness.
