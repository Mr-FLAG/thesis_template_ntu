\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work} % Main chapter title
\label{ch:Con}

In this thesis, we have studied three scheduling problems for distributed job execution: max-min fair resource allocation with task transmission for distributed job execution, efficient task scheduling with transmission for distributed job execution, and long-term fairness and efficiency trade-off for distributed job execution. The main contributions of the thesis including defining the fair resource allocation policy DAMF in distributed job execution with task transmission, presenting an algorithm for task assignment and transmission to improve the job processing efficiency in distributed job execution, and an algorithm for improving the job processing efficiency while providing a completion deadline guarantee in distributed job execution.

In Chapter \ref{ch:DAMF}, we have studied the fair resource allocation policies with task transmission for distributed job execution. We present a resource allocation policy named Dynamic Aggregate Max-min Fairness (DAMF), which is achievable and satisfies extensively recognized fairness properties of Pareto Efficiency, envy-freeness and strategy-proofness. We further propose algorithms to implement DAMF as well as a fast approximation to it. Experiments using real-world job traces demonstrate that compared with baseline policies, DAMF and its approximation algorithm significantly reduces the imbalance of resource allocation across jobs and further improves the average job response time, especially when the load distribution of jobs are highly skewed among sites.

In Chapter \ref{ch:WAST}, we have studied improving job processing efficiency via task scheduling with transmission in distributed job execution case. Noticing the mutual effect of job scheduling and task transmission, we begin by optimizing the response time of a single job first and transform the problem into solving multiple minimum-cost flow programs. After that, we develop an algorithm named Workload-aware Selection with Transmission (WAST), which iteratively schedules the job of the earliest completion time while taking task transmission into consideration. In addition, we present a fast approximation to WAST with marginal performance degradation. Experimental results show that WAST and its approximation significantly decreases the average job response time compared with baseline policies.

In Chapter \ref{ch:PFSP}, we have studied the long-term fairness and efficiency trade-off in distributed job execution. Inspired by the notion of protective scheduling algorithms in the single site case, we propose that the criterion on long-term fairness across jobs for distributed job execution should be that every job will finish before it finishes under the distributed Processor Sharing (PS) policy. Based on this, we construct two types of the slack system that help us judge the system protectiveness: a global view $GS$ scheme and an independent view $IS$ scheme. We further extend the protective algorithms FSP, PFSP and OFSP in the single site case to our distributed job execution based on these two schemes. We prove that although the violation can be rare, any globally order-based scheduling policies are not protective in the distributed job execution case. To address this, we further present PFSP\_x, a novel heuristic scheduling framework that adopts the $IS$ scheme to ensure protectiveness while adopting a hint job order derived by some efficiency-targeted policies. Our experiment demonstrates that PFSP\_x achieves the highest job processing efficiency among all policies that guarantee protectiveness, and PFSP using the $GS$ scheme can effectively decrease the number of jobs violating their PS deadline while keep a close job processing efficiency with those efficiency-targeted policies.

Following the research presented in this dissertation, several promising directions for future work can be outlined. A highly potential avenue is to extend the proposed fair resource allocation and efficient job scheduling policies to multi-resource environments within geo-distributed systems. While our current frameworks primarily focus on computing resources (i.e., slots) and treat network bandwidth as an auxiliary factor to enhance slot utilization, real-world distributed platforms inherently manage a heterogeneous mix of distinct resource types, including CPU, memory, and disk storage. Consequently, jobs typically submit tasks with multidimensional resource requirements, and developing a joint multi-resource allocation paradigm targeting at fairness and/or efficiency under network constraints would introduce a substantial advancement in distributed job execution. Another limitation of our work is that in we assume precise prediction on job's resource demand, processing times and system network resources. Although the performance of our proposed algorithms under unstable bandwidths has been evaluated, the practical viability of these policies could be further enhanced by incorporating prediction inaccuracies regarding job demands and arrival patterns directly into the system modeling phase. Proactively accounting for such mis-estimations would allow the development of more robust, uncertainty-aware scheduling strategies, thereby significantly bridging the gap between theoretical models and real-world deployments. In addition, one potential limitation of our work on the long-term fairness and efficiency trade-off is that the network resource in the system is ignored. Although incorporating the slack system with network resource can be difficult (transferring load across sites is possible to result in negative slack and violate protectiveness), protective scheduling policies allowing task transmissions are able to achieve further job processing efficiency improvement by transmissions, meanwhile keeping each job to finish before it finishes virtually under the PS policy.

Another promising direction is the application of learning-based approaches to distributed job execution with task transmission. While several reinforcement learning (RL) methods have been proposed for resource allocation and job scheduling \cite{wang2021incorporating, li2022drl, mohajer2024dynamic, wei2022multi}, most existing works primarily optimize job processing efficiency under overly simplified system models (e.g., assuming a single active job in the system or treating transmission costs as negligible by allowing arbitrary task placement). In contrast, our target environment demands that the central controller continuously aggregates diverse runtime state information, including task-level resource demands, execution times, data chunk sizes, spatial task distributions, alongside site capacities and network bandwidths. This holistic view introduces an exponentially large optimization space, posing a severe scalability challenge. Nevertheless, historical job arrival and workload patterns in specific geo-distributed systems can be leveraged to train customized RL algorithms tailored to address this complex problem.
